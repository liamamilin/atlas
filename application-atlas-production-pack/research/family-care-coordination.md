# Research Notes — Family Care Coordination

Directory location: §29 Home, Family, Personal & Local Services (siblings: Family Organizer, Babysitting Marketplace, Childcare Management System, Parenting / Baby Tracking Application, Home Management Application, Home Services Marketplace; also adjacent: Care Coordination Platform in §22 Healthcare).

Research date: 2026-09-07

## Research Goal

Understand what a Family Care Coordination application actually is by studying real products: what the central organizing object is, who participates, how care work is expressed and claimed, what records are kept, how the circle communicates, and where the boundary lies against neighboring Types (Family Organizer, Babysitting Marketplace, healthcare Care Coordination Platform, Parenting/Baby Tracking).

## Initial Boundary (hypothesis before research)

- Core purpose: coordinate the care of one person who needs care (aging parent, chronically ill or disabled relative, person after surgery, child with special needs, sometimes a new mother) among a circle of family/friends/professional helpers.
- Users: a primary family caregiver who organizes; extended family and friends who help; sometimes paid/professional carers; the care recipient directly or represented.
- Likely confusions:
  - Family Organizer (household logistics for a co-resident family) — closest sibling in §29.
  - Babysitting Marketplace / Home Services Marketplace — two-sided stranger matching.
  - Care Coordination Platform (§22) — same words "care coordination", but provider-side clinical.
  - Childcare Management / Daycare Management — business-side management of a facility.
  - Parenting / Baby Tracking — tracking a baby's feeding/sleep, no helper circle.
- Unknowns going in: is the care circle the container or is the family the container? Is a needs/sign-up loop definitional? What privacy/role structures exist? Do professional carers participate inside the product?

## Research Questions

1. What is the central object: care recipient, care circle, calendar, or task list?
2. How are care needs expressed and claimed (tasks, calendar slots, meal trains, "who can help?")?
3. What roles exist (coordinator, helper, recipient, professional) and what can each do?
4. How is membership constructed (invitation, shared code, search) and how private is the space?
5. What care records are maintained (medications, documents, care plans, journals)?
6. How does the circle stay informed (updates, announcements, messages, well-wishes)?
7. What is the lifecycle: setup → ongoing coordination → wind-down (bereavement, recovery)?
8. Where exactly is the boundary vs Family Organizer, vs care marketplaces, vs healthcare care coordination?

## Representative Products

Chosen for market representativeness, document completeness, different product philosophies (consumer app vs community web service vs volunteer signup-sheet vs service-augmented platform), and different eras (2002 web tool → 2026 AI-era app):

1. **Caring Village** (caringvillage.com) — consumer mobile+web caregiver app, care-recipient-centered "village", richest record-keeping (medications, documents, care plans, wellness journal), free + paid plans.
2. **Lotsa Helping Hands** (lotsahelpinghands.com) — long-lived community web service ("care teams"), care calendar with task sign-up, well wishes, announcements; official knowledge base on Help Scout.
3. **CareCalendar** (carecalendar.org) — the oldest sample (©2002), free volunteer-oriented signup-sheet web tool organized by a coordinator; Calendar ID + Security Code access; serves as the historical / market-sample check anchor.
4. **ianacare** (ianacare.com) — caregiver app ("family care navigation") with a service layer: human Care Navigators funded through employers, health plans, and the Medicare GUIDE program; app retains a family team/circle coordination core.

### Product Mismatch / unreachable samples (recorded, abandoned)

- **care.ly** — redirects to Caregiving.com (a caregiver content site); the eldercare coordination product of that name is not reachable. Abandoned after 1 fetch.

## Product Observations

### Caring Village (evidence A unless noted)

- Positioning: "The #1 Caregiver App for Family Care Coordination"; "Your Family's Caregiving Command Center"; "the only app built specifically for family caregivers."
- Container: the **Village** — "Each village centers on one loved one." A family caring for two aging parents runs "two separate Villages." The village is explicitly the "Command Center for all of your caregiving activities."
- Setup flow (documented as 3 steps): 1. Create Your Village (sign up free, centered around the loved one) → 2. Invite Your Circle ("Add family, friends, and care professionals with role-based permissions") → 3. Coordinate Care Together ("Share tasks, schedules, medications, and updates — all in one secure place").
- Seven advertised features: 1 Village, 2 Care Plans (create care plans; active care plans view), 3 Calendar (create events, assign members), 4 Medications (add medications with photo and reasons; "today's medications"; smart reminders), 5 Documents (document storage with folders; "important documents safe, secure, and accessible"), 6 Messaging ("chat privately and securely with loved ones"), 7 To-Dos ("shareable to dos").
- Wellness Journal: "Post updates, log daily check-ins, track mood and wellness trends, and keep the whole village informed — family, friends, neighbors, and care professionals all stay connected through one shared timeline."
- AI assistant: "Julia, Your AI Virtual Caregiver" — 24/7 questions, guidance, "turn health updates into actionable next steps" (current-generation add-on; not part of older structure).
- Users visible in official testimonials: daughter/primary caregiver, son & care coordinator, husband-and-wife caring for mother, and a professional caregiver "managing multiple clients" inside the app.
- Stated pain points addressed: missed appointments, missed medication doses, scattered care information, weak family ties, emotional and logistical stress.
- Privacy posture: encryption, "never sold or shared with third parties."
- Business model: free tier + paid plans (pricing page exists; exact tiering not examined).

### Lotsa Helping Hands (evidence A unless noted)

- Positioning: "Easily Organize Help for Someone in Need. A central place to coordinate meals and help for friends & family." Also "Start a Care Team" / "Create a community."
- Container: the **community / care team** — created for "someone in need"; one testimonial describes six siblings coordinating care for an elderly mother across states.
- Audience segments named on the homepage: Caregivers ("You need help organizing care for a loved one"), Friends & Family, Volunteers ("help people in your neighborhood"), New Parents ("let family know how they can help").
- Four advertised features:
  - **Care Calendar** — "Schedule and sign up for tasks such as meal delivery and rides to appointments."
  - **Well Wishes** — "Drop a line to say hello or offer support to the family."
  - **Announcements** — "Keep your team updated on progress and other ways to help."
  - **Photo Gallery** — "Post or view personal photos and share a memory or milestone."
- Knowledge base structure confirms roles and mechanics:
  - Collections: Member Questions / Administrator Questions.
  - Administrator categories: Add Site Admin/groups; **Adding Members, Creating Groups, and Assigning Roles**; Announcements and Emailing; **Calendar and Tasks**; Creating a Community.
  - Setup guidance ("What Do I Do After I Create a Community?"): add Admins and/or **Site Organizers** to help set up (add tasks, invite members); add an Announcement describing the purpose of the community; add **tasks where help is needed** or upcoming occasions (birthday, anniversary); **invite Members via email or text**.
  - A "Join a Care Team" community search exists for joining existing teams (join flow alongside invitation).
- Value claims: Coordination ("Quickly schedule meals, rides, and appointments for someone in need"), Communication ("Send encouragement and keep your support team in the loop").
- Longevity: help center documents "Changes from Old Lotsa Helping Hands Site" — the product has existed across platform generations.
- Business model: free.

### CareCalendar (evidence A unless noted)

- Positioning: "The original free website to organize meals and other help for families during a time of illness, recovery or a life changing event, such as the birth of a baby or death of a family member." Copyright 2002–2026.
- Container: a **CareCalendar page** ("Start a new CareCalendar page"); a coordinator can "Manage one or many separate CareCalendar pages with ease."
- Core loop, stated verbatim on the homepage: "When a loved one needs help, simply list the help that is needed. Family and friends can then sign up to fill those needs. Like an online signup sheet, only better."
- Three documented roles:
  - **Coordinators**: easily coordinate sign-ups; eliminate "phone-tag" and email-overload; add/edit/delete needs quickly; manage one or many pages.
  - **Helpers**: see what is needed and when; sign up online anytime; fill the needs that fit their schedule; see what meals others are planning; maps and driving directions; email reminders; send gift cards, gifts, greeting cards.
  - **Recipients**: "When someone asks how they can help, simply direct them to your CareCalendar page"; email reminders tell who and what to expect; keep everyone up-to-date with photos and news updates.
- Access model: logon by **Calendar ID + Security Code** — page-scoped access without a full user account (shown directly on the homepage logon widget).
- Need types: Meals, Rides, Visits, Errands, Yardwork, Housework.
- Use cases: New Moms/Babies, Cancer Patients, Respite Care, Deployment, Alzheimers, Surgery.
- Used by: Churches, Neighbors, Co-workers, Support Groups, Extended Family, Bible Study Groups.
- Vendor-claimed counters (marketing page, not independently verified): 280,000+ pages, 6 million meals scheduled, 2 million visits, 650,000 rides, 2.6 million other needs.

### ianacare (evidence A unless noted; testimonial quotes are official-site-published user quotes)

- Positioning: "The platform for family care navigation." "Coordinate care, rally your circle, and carry the load together."
- Container: the **team / circle** — an official testimonial: "I had 30 family members on my ianacare team, so everyone received the same information at one time"; another: the community was "handpicked who is going to be a part of that community."
- Core need-broadcast loop, from an official testimonial: "Through the app, you can just push a button and say, 'Who can help?' with a particular need — such as sitting with my dad or taking him to an appointment. It brings everyone together to help you more easily ask for support."
- Calendar: "The calendar feature of the app is super helpful when keeping multiple individuals on the same page or coordinating family coverage of events" (official testimonial).
- Messaging/updates: team-wide messages; users report re-reading messages after the care recipient's death (bereavement use of the persistent record).
- Service layer (distinctive to this vendor): dedicated human **Care Navigator** (video/call/message), 24/7 hotline, content library, support groups, AI companion ("iana(TM) AI Care Companion"); funded as an employee/health-plan benefit and via the Medicare GUIDE dementia program ("Check with your insurance or employer if ianacare is a covered benefit"). Families use it free in that channel.
- Care situations named: dementia (GUIDE program), aging parents, chronic conditions, disability, cancer, sandwich generation ("caring for kids and aging parents"), bereavement.

### What repeats across all four (candidates for the defining structure, evidence B)

1. A coordination container organized around **one care recipient / one care situation** ("someone in need", "one loved one", a family page/team).
2. **Invited, membership-gated participation by already-known people** — family, friends, neighbors, congregations, and sometimes professional carers; never stranger matching.
3. A **needs → claim/assign → done loop**: the coordinator or recipient posts what help is needed (typically dated — meals, rides, visits, errands, sitting, appointments), helpers see the gaps and volunteer/claim, and fulfillment is visible to everyone.
4. **Circle-wide updates/communication** — journal, announcements, well wishes, messages, photos — so everyone holds the same picture without phone-tag.
5. A **coordinator function** (create the circle, describe the situation, invite, post needs) — present in all four, though named differently.

### Common but not defining (evidence B for presence, not for definition)

- medication lists with reminders (1 of 4)
- document storage (1 of 4)
- care plans (1 of 4)
- wellness/mood journaling (1 of 4)
- photo galleries, well-wishes/encouragement surfaces (2 of 4)
- email reminders, maps/directions, gift cards (older community features)
- content library, support groups, hotline (service-augmented pole)
- AI assistants (current-generation, 2 of 4)

## Canonical Abstraction

### L0 — Defining Invariant (deliberately small)

A Family Care Coordination application is recognizable only if it has all four:

1. **Care-recipient-centered coordination container** — one shared space organized around a specific person who needs care (or a specific care situation), not around a household, a team, or a marketplace listing. Remove → family organizer / task app.
2. **Invited private circle of known helpers** — membership is gated (invitation, shared code, handpick); participants are already-known people (including cross-household relatives and volunteers), not discovered strangers. Remove → care marketplace or social platform.
3. **The express-and-claim care loop** — care needs (typically dated occasions: meals, rides, visits, sitting, appointments) are listed by the coordinator or recipient, claimed/assigned by helpers, and fulfillment is visible to the circle. Remove → generic messenger or journal.
4. **Circle-wide awareness** — updates about the person and the care situation flow to all members on a shared surface (journal/announcements/messages), persisting over time. Remove → a bare signup sheet with no communication role.

The coordinator role and the shared care calendar are realized inside these four (a container needs a creator; the care loop is typically time-placed), so they are not separate L0 items.

### L1 — Common Mature Structure

- shared care calendar as the primary when-surface
- coordinator/admin role distinct from ordinary member
- reminders/notifications for needs and occasions
- member management (groups, roles) and situation announcement at setup
- photo sharing and encouragement surfaces (well wishes)
- records useful across a care journey (some products: medications, documents)

### L2 — Variant / Optional Structure

- record breadth: full caregiving "command center" (medications + documents + care plans + journal) vs lightweight meal-train signup sheet
- organizer identity: nuclear-family pole vs community/church/volunteer pole (CareCalendar's churches/Bible-study groups; Lotsa's volunteers and new-parent meal trains)
- professional participation: professional carers invited as circle members (Caring Village testimonial) vs a funded human navigation service layered on top (ianacare Navigators via employer/health-plan/medicare benefit)
- AI assistance (Caring Village Julia, ianacare AI companion)
- access model: full accounts vs page-scoped Calendar ID + security code
- monetization: free, freemium, benefit-funded B2B2C
- join model: invitation-only vs searchable public teams (Lotsa offers both)

### L3 — Vendor-specific (stays in Research Notes)

- "Village" naming and the seven-feature command-center layout (Caring Village)
- "Well Wishes" / "Site Organizer" terminology (Lotsa)
- Calendar ID + Security Code exact mechanics and vendor-claimed scale counters (CareCalendar)
- "Julia" AI persona (Caring Village); "iana(TM) AI Care Companion" (ianacare)
- Medicare GUIDE program specifics, respite benefit amounts, eligibility flows (ianacare)
- CareCalendar gift shop (gift cards/greeting cards)

### Rejected Findings (considered and excluded from the core)

- **Phone/app-first**: CareCalendar (2002, web, no smartphone assumption) fits perfectly; the core predates mobile.
- **Accounts**: CareCalendar works with Calendar ID + security code; account-based identity is a common implementation, not definitional.
- **Medications/documents/care plans**: only the richest sampled product has them; the Type exists without them.
- **Health-data handling as a defining property**: care is mostly logistical/social; medical records are not managed as clinical data of record (that would be the healthcare side).
- **Payments between members**: absent across the sample; money flows (gift cards) are peripheral and product-specific.
- **AI assistance**: current-generation add-on only.

## Historical / Market-Sample Check (older, regional, platform-native products)

CareCalendar (2002) is the deliberate historical anchor: pre-smartphone, account-light, volunteer/church organized — and it satisfies the four L0 properties exactly. The pre-digital analog (paper meal-train and church sign-up sheets with a coordinator phoning helpers) maps one-to-one onto the same structure. Therefore the core must not include mobile apps, accounts, medications, documents, AI, or photo galleries. The definition also holds for non-US community forms (congregations, co-workers, support groups) since the container is the care situation, not any national benefit system.

## Boundary Findings

1. **vs Family Organizer (§29 sibling)** — discriminator: what the container is centered on. Family Organizer centers the *household* (co-resident family: chores, shopping, kids' activities, a family calendar); Family Care Coordination centers *one person who needs care*, and the circle deliberately crosses household boundaries (siblings in other states, friends, neighbors, congregations, professionals). Removing the care-recipient-centered container and its helper circle leaves a family organizer; removing household logistics while keeping the care circle leaves this Type. Edge: Lotsa's "New Parents" and meal trains — still organized around "someone in need", so they stay here.
2. **vs Babysitting Marketplace / Home Services Marketplace (§29)** — discriminator: matching strangers with transaction/booking semantics vs mobilizing already-known people with no matching marketplace. CareCalendar's own words: "When someone asks how they can help, simply direct them to your CareCalendar page." The prior babysitting-marketplace research notes recorded the same seam from the other side ("internal family logistics around existing carers, not two-sided stranger matching").
3. **vs Care Coordination Platform (§22 Healthcare)** — same two words, opposite operator: healthcare care coordination is provider/payer-operated on patient populations with clinical workflows; this Type is consumer/family-operated on one loved one with informal care. The only bridge in the sample is ianacare's benefit-funded service layer — the family-facing product remains a coordination app, so ianacare stays here with the service layer recorded as a variant.
4. **vs Patient Portal / Telehealth (§22)** — no clinical system of record, no provider workflow; the app may *reference* appointments and medications but does not manage them as clinical data.
5. **vs Childcare Management System / Daycare Management (§29)** — those manage a care *business* (enrollment, staff, billing, classrooms) for many children; this Type coordinates unpaid/circle care for one person. A daycare portal for parents is a different operator and unit.
6. **vs Parenting / Baby Tracking Application (§29)** — baby tracking logs the child's feeding/sleep for the parents' own use; no helper circle and no needs-claiming loop. CareCalendar's "New Moms" use case (meal train around a birth) belongs here, not there.
7. **vs a pure health-journal/update service** (e.g. CaringBridge-style) — a product with only the updates surface and no needs-claiming/calendar loop would drift toward a communication/journal Type; the express-and-claim loop is what keeps a product in this Type. Recorded as a soft edge; no such leaf exists in the directory.

## Taxonomy Notes

- No directory change recommended: the leaf is a coherent Type, distinct from all §29 siblings by the care-recipient-centered container + circle + claim loop.
- Potential reader confusion: the directory also contains "Care Coordination Platform" (§22). The two names overlap; discriminators are documented above. Not a boundary error — different operators, different units of coordination — but worth keeping in mind for cross-references.
- ianacare's employer/health-plan distribution and human Navigator service stretch the Type toward service delivery; classified as a variant (service-augmented pole), consistent with the directory keeping one leaf.

## Uncertainties

- Fine-grained permission ladders (what exactly a helper may edit vs view per product) are only partially documented; role-based permissions are confirmed in one product, coordinator/member/admin mechanics in another, code-based access in a third. Treated as implementation variance; no precise permission claims made in the final document.
- Whether a calendar *view* (vs any dated needs list) is required: all four samples have one, but the signup-sheet form shows the dated needs list is the substance; final document treats the calendar as the common realization of the care loop's time dimension.
- Vendor-claimed scale numbers (pages, meals, users) are marketing figures; not independently verified; excluded from the final document.
- Lotsa's "Join a Care Team" search implies semi-public team discovery for some teams; extent unclear; recorded as product-specific join-model variance.
- The professional-caregiver-as-member pattern rests on one published testimonial (Caring Village); plausible but marked single-product evidence.

## Final Synthesis

A Family Care Coordination application is a private, care-recipient-centered coordination space for the circle of already-known people who help care for one person who needs care. Its defining structure is four-part: a container centered on the care recipient; invited membership crossing household boundaries; an express-and-claim loop turning care needs (typically dated occasions) into claimed, visible commitments; and circle-wide updates that keep everyone equally informed. Around this core, mature products add the shared care calendar, coordinator roles, reminders, encouragement and photo surfaces, and — at the richest pole — medication, document, and care-plan records. Variants range from the 2002-era volunteer signup sheet to today's AI-assisted caregiving command centers and benefit-funded service-augmented platforms. The Type is bounded against household organizers (container centered on the household, not a care recipient), care marketplaces (stranger matching vs mobilizing known people), and healthcare care coordination (family-operated informal care vs provider-operated clinical coordination).
